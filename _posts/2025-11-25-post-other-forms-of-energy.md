## Einstein's Equation of Energy
Back in the 20th century, Albert Einstein developed a famous formula for calculating a form of energy called mass energy. The formula is $E = mc^2$, where $E$ is the energy in joules, $m$ is the mass, and $c$ is the speed of light. This means that the more mass an object has, the more energy it has. An object with a mass of 35 kilograms will have an energy of approximately $3.15 \times 10^{18}$ joules, while an object with a mass of 30 kilograms will have an energy of approximately $2.7 \times 10^{18}$ joules. As you can see, the 35 kilogram object has a greater energy. However, there is another way to prove this statement. The speed of light is a positive constant, so the bigger number you are multiply it to, the bigger result you will get. For example, if you multiply every number by 5, the bigger the number you are multiplying 5 with, the bigger answer you will get. This formula shows that mass can be converted to energy.

## Six Conservations
There are two other conservation laws related to the conservation of energy. The first one is the conservation of linear momentum. The other is called the conservation of angular momentum. Sadly, these will not be covered in this post. There are another three conservations included here. The first one is the conservation of charge, which is the law that you can count all the positive charges you have, and subtract it by all the negative charges you have. The next one, the conservation of baryons states that the number of baryons, particles such as neutrons and protons in a closed system will remain the same every time. And, finally, we have the conservation of leptons. If we count the number of leptons, another group of particles, we will get the same number before and after a reaction. These are the main six conservation laws of physics.
